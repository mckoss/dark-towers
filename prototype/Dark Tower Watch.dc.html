Airliners are landing at airports where nobody is in the tower — or there is no tower at all.

Across the country, airports with regular passenger service have control towers that close for the night — and some have no tower at all. The flights keep coming. With nobody in the tower, no one is directing traffic: pilots announce themselves on a shared radio channel and are expected to see each other to avoid collisions. This site counts those flights and publishes every pair of aircraft that came closer than a controller would have allowed.

Method

What this site counts, and how.

Many smaller airports have control towers that are staffed only part of the day, and some have no tower at all. Those hours, where they exist, were set when the airport had no passenger flights. They have not changed. The passenger flights have arrived. This site does not argue that from anecdote — it counts the flights.

Sources
FlightAware — arrival and departure records, and the position signals each aircraft broadcasts.
FAA Chart Supplement — the official published tower hours for each airport.
Published airline schedules — to tell passenger flights apart from private ones.
OpenStreetMap and CARTO — basemap tiles.
Step one

Collect every flight while the tower is closed

For each night we list every arrival and departure between the tower closing and reopening the next morning, in local time. Where an airport has no tower, we use the hours a tower would ordinarily be staffed nearby. Flights are grouped by the evening they belong to, so a 1 a.m. landing is filed under the night before.

Step two

Keep only the paths close to the airport

Each flight's path is trimmed to the positions within 10 nautical miles of the airport — about 11.5 road miles — and within the hours the tower was closed. Flights that pass nearby at other times are dropped.

Step three

Measure how close aircraft came to each other

Positions are lined up to a common clock and every pair of aircraft is measured. When two aircraft are less than 3 nautical miles apart and less than 1,000 feet apart vertically at the same moment, we flag it.

The standard
With a controller on duty, aircraft are kept at least 3 nautical miles apart, or 1,000 feet apart vertically. When the tower is closed, nothing enforces this safety margin.

What a flagged event is, and what it is not

A flag means two aircraft were closer than a controller would have allowed. It is not a finding that either pilot did anything wrong, and it is not an official ruling of any kind. When the tower is closed, pilots are legally permitted to fly closer than these distances as long as they see each other to avoid collisions. That is the point: an airliner coming in to land and a training aircraft circling the field share the same air with nothing between them but a radio call and a pair of eyes.

Known limitations

Aircraft that do not broadcast a position signal do not appear at all, so these counts are a floor, not a total.
Broadcast altitude is not corrected for local air pressure, so altitude figures carry roughly ±100 feet.
Landing and takeoff times are sometimes estimated rather than measured; where the measured time is missing, the estimate is used.
Coverage close to the ground is uneven, so the lowest parts of some flight paths are missing.
Who we are

A citizen project, built on public data.

Dark Tower Watch is not run by the FAA, an airport, an airline, or any government body. It is the work of two residents who live under these flight paths. Everything on the site is drawn from publicly available ADS-B position data — the same signals aircraft broadcast for anyone to receive — and from published tower hours and airline schedules. Nothing here is confidential, and none of it required special access.

If you see something we have wrong, tell us. Corrections improve the record.
